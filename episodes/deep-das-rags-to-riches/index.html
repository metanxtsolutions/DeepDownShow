Deep Das: 5 Businesses in 6 Years, Rags to Riches

Serial entrepreneur Deep Das built five running businesses in six years starting in 2017. He walks Barun through each one and explains how he manages them all without burning out.

Most entrepreneurs spend years finding their one idea. Deep Das, the guest on episode 12 of the Deep Down Show, has five running at once: a gym, a school, a wedding-and-banquet venue, a car detailing studio, and a warehouse-rental company. He tells host Barun that all five were built from scratch within six years, starting in 2017, and the conversation is less about any single success story than about how one person keeps that many plates spinning without any of them cracking.

Das doesn't come across as a formula-chaser. Each business, as he explains it, grew out of something personal, his own gym habit turning into Urja Gym, a lodge he took over turning into a wedding venue, his attention to grooming turning into a car spa. What ties the episode together is less the businesses themselves than his repeated insistence on patience, on visiting every site himself, and on not copying what worked for someone else without understanding why it worked.

The conversation also doubles as a mini business-strategy discussion, with Barun pushing Das on why aggressive, PayTM-style growth can backfire, and how someone with an idea but no capital should actually get started. Das answers both from experience rather than theory, since by his own account he's the one who had to work out the mistakes in real time.

From the gym floor to Urja Gym

Das says his first venture, started in 2017, was Urja Gym, born directly out of his own habit of working out. Spending time at the gym meant meeting bodybuilders and trainers, and that exposure, he says, is what nudged him from being a gym-goer to being a gym owner. He picked the name Urja deliberately: it means "energy" in Bengali, and he wanted a name that carried that meaning rather than an imported English one.

Running the gym also meant learning to manage a mixed crowd calmly, dealing with different kinds of members without friction. On the business side, he describes a seasonal pattern common to fitness businesses: interest spikes before certain months and drops off afterward. To smooth that out, Urja Gym ran a low-cost entry promotion he refers to as "Gym at Rs 1", a nominal admission offer timed to the off-season to pull in members before applying the regular admission fee and monthly charges.

St. Mary's Public School: activities, culture, and a name people questioned

Das's second venture was St. Mary's Public School, and he tells Barun his goal from the start wasn't just academics. He wanted a school where every kind of activity and every kind of culture had room, not a school built purely around exam results. English is taught as a subject at the school, and he describes trying to raise the grassroots level of preparation for students rather than assuming families already had it.

One recurring question he says he's faced while travelling to different areas is about the school's name: people have told him a name like St. Mary's sounds Christian, and questioned why he'd choose it, even though the school isn't a missionary or religiously affiliated institution. His response, as he frames it to Barun, is that the school is deliberately inclusive: children from different religious backgrounds eat together, celebrate festivals together, and families are invited to participate in school programmes regardless of background.

He also brings up the COVID-era lockdown as a turning point for the school. Well-established institutions in cities, he says, already knew how to run things digitally, but in the areas where his school operates, getting children and their families ready for online classes, in front of a camera, on a schedule, was a genuinely difficult adjustment.

On the question of franchising the school model, Das is skeptical of treating it as a copy-paste template. He compares it to trying to open a "franchise" of the Ramakrishna Mission: some things, he argues, are singular by design, built on an infrastructure and a culture that can't simply be replicated by handing someone a license. If he ever does franchise, he says a franchise partner needs to be treated like an actual partner, not just handed a name to run alone.

JD Palace: a lodge that became a banquet hall, and marriage on EMI

JD Palace, Das's next venture, started life as an existing lodge business that he took over and rebuilt into a banquet and wedding venue. Describing how he approaches customers, he says his goal is to serve people without stretching them beyond their pocket, whatever their budget looks like.

That philosophy shows up directly in how JD Palace prices weddings: Das offers an EMI-style installment option, so families can pay for a wedding function in parts rather than one lump sum, a model he compares to how jewellery shops let customers pay for gold in installments. It's a practical answer to a real constraint, weddings are expensive and often front-loaded, and Das positions it as one of the venue's selling points, alongside the confidence of customers who, in his telling, call it simply "the best."

The Car Spa and Akash Enterprise: grooming cars, renting the sky

The Car Spa grew out of a comparison Das draws directly: just as people take care of their own bodies and appearance, he reasons, a car deserves the same kind of upkeep. That logic, applied to detailing and car care, became his third venture.

Das's other venture, Akash Enterprise, rents out warehouse space to companies that need it. He explains the name deliberately: Akash means "sky," and the idea behind it is something boundless, you can't put a wall around the sky. The business came out of noticing demand from companies that needed warehousing but didn't want to build or own it themselves, so Akash Enterprise provides that space instead.

Running five businesses without losing the thread

Asked how he keeps five different businesses running at once, Das doesn't describe a single management trick. He says he tries to personally visit every location rather than manage everything remotely, because being physically present is how he catches problems, and opportunities, that reports alone would miss. Within each business, he delegates daily operations to his teams but stays close enough to step in when needed, and he stresses patience, both with staff who are still learning and with customers who need time to be convinced.

On team building specifically, he tells Barun that getting a team invested matters more than instructions: whether someone is a business owner or an entrepreneur, the real test is whether the people working under them actually care about the outcome, not just the paycheck.

Why PayTM struggled, in Das's reading

The episode's clearest strategy discussion comes when Barun brings up PayTM. Das's take is that a business chasing market dominance, becoming what he calls the "market king", by expanding fast and diversifying into new areas without keeping its fundamentals and margins in order eventually runs into serious trouble once market conditions shift. He points to Amazon as a contrast, framing it as a company that focused on building up its core product and service before expanding into other areas, such as payments, rather than diversifying first and worrying about fundamentals later. The broader point he draws for other entrepreneurs is to have a clear strategy for using investment and growth capital, rather than spending it purely to capture territory.

Have an idea, no money: Das's answer

Toward the end of the conversation, Das addresses a question he says he gets often: what should someone do if they have a business idea but no capital to start it? His advice leans on proximity to people already doing it: follow, observe and learn from entrepreneurs who are ahead of you, rather than waiting until conditions are perfect. He mentions being a committee member of the Bengal Business Council and actively helping bring in new members, treating that network itself as part of how ideas turn into businesses.

He closes the episode with a simple metaphor for the takeaway from his own journey: you can't know how deep water is by standing on the road and looking at it, you have to actually step into the water to find out. For Das, that's the difference between planning a business forever and starting one.

Who is Deep Das

Deep Das is a serial entrepreneur based in the Kolkata area who, starting in 2017, built five separate businesses within six years: Urja Gym, St. Mary's Public School, JD Palace, The Car Spa and Akash Enterprise, spanning fitness, education, hospitality, car care and warehousing. He is a committee member of the Bengal Business Council.

Why this conversation matters

Deep Das's episode isn't a single origin story so much as a working model for how one entrepreneur manages breadth without spreading too thin, visiting every site himself, pricing around what customers can actually afford, and refusing to copy a business model just because it worked somewhere else. That instinct to build around a real personal need, a gym habit, an inherited lodge, an eye for detail on a car, rather than a market trend, connects his story to Reeti Jana's account of rebuilding a family nursery in the corporate-experience episode, and his skepticism about franchising without real partnership echoes the questions Subhashis Dutt raises about Bengali business culture in the "why Bengalis don't do business" episode.

For anyone weighing whether to start one business or several, Das's answer isn't really about the number five. It's about being willing to step into the water on the first one before worrying about the rest.
